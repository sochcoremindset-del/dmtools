Understanding Board Feet: The Ultimate Guide for Woodworkers

If you've ever walked into a hardwood lumber yard in the US, you quickly realize they don't sell wood quite like the big box home centers do. Instead of pricing a piece of wood by the linear foot, rough hardwood is sold by volume. This specific measurement of volume is called a board foot (often abbreviated as BDFT or BF). Whether you are an experienced furniture maker, a general contractor, or a DIY weekend warrior, knowing how to calculate your board footage before stepping up to the cash register will save you time, confusion, and a whole lot of money.

What Exactly is a Board Foot?

A board foot is a specialized unit of measurement for lumber volume in North America. Simply put, one board foot is equal to a piece of wood that is exactly 12 inches long, 12 inches wide, and 1 inch thick. Mathematically, that equates to 144 cubic inches of wood.

The reason this measurement exists is because hardwood lumber is a three-dimensional material that comes in completely random widths and lengths. A board that is 1" thick, 6" wide, and 24" long contains the exact same amount of wood volume (one board foot) as a board that is 1" thick, 12" wide, and 12" long. Pricing wood by volume ensures you are paying a fair price for the actual yield of the tree, regardless of the board's unique shape.

The Board Foot Formula

You don't need a math degree to figure out your lumber needs. The standard industry formula is incredibly straightforward:

Board Feet = (Thickness in inches × Width in inches × Length in feet) ÷ 12

Note: If you prefer to measure your length in inches instead of feet, you simply divide the total by 144 instead of 12. Both equations will give you the exact same result.

Nominal vs. Actual Dimensions

The biggest mistake beginners make when calculating board feet is using the actual surfaced dimensions of the wood rather than the nominal dimensions. Lumber mills price wood based on the rough size of the board before it goes through the planer or jointer.

For example, if you buy a standard 2x4 stud at the hardware store, it actually measures 1.5 inches by 3.5 inches. However, when calculating board feet, you must use the nominal "2" and "4". Furthermore, hardwood thickness is traditionally expressed in quarters of an inch. A 1-inch thick rough board is called "4/4" (four-quarters). A 1.5-inch board is "6/4", and a 2-inch board is "8/4". When entering thickness into our calculator above, use the nominal decimal equivalent (e.g., enter 1.5 for 6/4 lumber).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a linear foot and a board foot?

A linear foot measures only the length of the board (a straight 12-inch line), ignoring how wide or thick the piece is. A board foot measures the total 3D volume of the board. Softwoods and trim moldings are usually sold by the linear foot, while rough hardwoods are sold by the board foot.

Should I account for waste when buying lumber?

Yes, absolutely. Because rough lumber often has knots, checks, or sapwood that you'll need to cut away, standard shop practice is to calculate your final project's required board footage and then add a 20% to 30% waste factor to your purchase order.
